# KLG Style Guide — Appellate Briefs and Professional Writing

**Version:** 1.0 (2026-07)
**Scope:** All court filings, memoranda, client communications, and AI-generated drafts.
**Authority:** Firm-wide. Deviations require supervising attorney sign-off.

---

## 1. Absolute Prohibitions

### 1.1 Banned Phrases

The following phrases are prohibited in all KLG writing. The skill checker flags every instance.

| Banned | Why | Preferred |
|--------|-----|-----------|
| "instant case" | Legalese; pompous | "this case" or "here" |
| "furthermore" | Throat-clearing transition | Cut it; restructure the sentence |
| "therefore" | Usually unnecessary | Cut or restructure |
| "therefrom" / "thereof" / "therein" | Archaic legalese | Rewrite with plain English |
| "clearly" | Asserts rather than proves | Delete; let the argument prove clarity |
| "it is axiomatic" | Pompous filler | State the rule directly |
| "it is well established that" | Filler before a proposition you should just assert | State the proposition directly |
| "as such" | Misused as a transition (correct use: referring back to a quality just stated) | "Therefore" when you mean consequence; usually just delete |
| "hereinabove" / "hereinbelow" | Archaic | "above" / "below" |
| "aforementioned" | Archaic | "the [noun] discussed above" or just repeat the noun |
| "needless to say" | If it's needless, cut the sentence | Cut the sentence |
| "it cannot be gainsaid" | Archaic | Delete |
| "at this juncture" | Filler | "now" or cut |
| "the undersigned" | Cold and bureaucratic | Use first person or the firm's name |

### 1.2 Doubled Modifiers

Do not stack redundant intensifiers. These are always wrong:
- "clearly and unambiguously" → "unambiguously"
- "completely and totally" → delete both
- "sole and exclusive" → "sole" or "exclusive"
- "null and void" → "void" (in California courts)
- "each and every" → "each" or "every"
- "true and correct" → "correct"
- "over and above" → "beyond"
- "unless and until" → "until" (if temporal) or "unless" (if conditional)

### 1.3 Empty Intensifiers

Delete these without replacement:
- "quite," "very," "really," "essentially," "basically," "generally"
- "in essence," "in fact," "as a matter of fact"

## 2. Punctuation

### 2.1 Em Dash

**No spaces.** The em dash (—) sits flush against both surrounding words.

- Wrong: `The court held that the rule — if it applied — barred recovery.`
- Right: `The court held that the rule—if it applied—barred recovery.`

Use an em dash for a sharp interruption or parenthetical. Use commas or parentheses for softer asides. Do not use an en dash (–) where an em dash is called for.

### 2.2 Serial (Oxford) Comma

Always use the serial comma before "and" or "or" in a list of three or more items.

- Wrong: `The court reviewed the motion, opposition and reply.`
- Right: `The court reviewed the motion, opposition, and reply.`

### 2.3 Quotation Marks

- Periods and commas go inside closing quotation marks (American style).
- Semicolons and colons go outside.
- Question marks go inside only if they are part of the quoted material.

### 2.4 Ellipsis

Use the ellipsis character (…) or three spaced periods (. . .) consistently within a document. Do not omit material that changes the meaning of a quoted passage; bracket additions with [ ].

## 3. Voice, Tone, and Clarity

### 3.1 Active Voice

Prefer active constructions. Passive voice is acceptable only when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.

- Passive: `The motion was denied by the trial court.`
- Active: `The trial court denied the motion.`

### 3.2 No Nominalizations or Gerunds as Subjects

Convert noun-heavy constructions to active verbs.

- Nominalization: `The court's determination of the issue was that the defendant prevailed.`
- Active: `The court determined that the defendant prevailed.`

- Gerund chain: `By making a finding that the contract was ambiguous, the court enabled consideration of extrinsic evidence.`
- Active: `Because the court found the contract ambiguous, it admitted extrinsic evidence.`

### 3.3 No Throat-Clearing

Cut any opening that delays the substantive point.

- Throat-clearing: `It is important to note that the trial court made several critical errors.`
- Direct: `The trial court made three reversible errors.`

Other patterns to delete:
- "It should be noted that…"
- "It bears noting that…"
- "It is worth observing that…"
- "As the court below recognized…" (just say what the court recognized)

### 3.4 No Italics or Bold for Emphasis

In brief prose, emphasis comes from word choice and sentence structure—not typography. Reserve:
- **Bold** for point headings and section labels only.
- *Italics* for case names, titles of publications, and foreign-language phrases only.

## 4. Argument Structure

### 4.1 Point Headings

Point headings must:
1. State a conclusion, not a question or a topic.
2. Be written in all caps (or sentence-case if house style uses that for California briefs—confirm with supervising attorney).
3. End without a period.
4. Be one or two sentences maximum.
5. Be independently persuasive—the reader should understand the argument from the heading alone.

- Weak (topic): `THE STANDARD OF REVIEW`
- Strong (conclusion): `THE COURT REVIEWS DE NOVO BECAUSE THE TRIAL COURT DECIDED A PURE QUESTION OF LAW`

### 4.2 Standard of Review

State the standard of review at the outset of each major argument section—one sentence, then move on. Do not bury it.

### 4.3 Lead with the Strongest Argument

Open with your strongest ground. Do not save it for last. Courts read with diminishing attention.

### 4.4 One Argument per Point Heading

Do not combine independent legal theories under one heading. Each theory gets its own heading and developed analysis.

### 4.5 Statement of Facts

- Cite the record on every factual claim: `(CT 47)` for clerk's transcript, `(RT 123:14–124:2)` for reporter's transcript.
- No argument in the Statement of Facts.
- Chronological order unless a different structure serves the narrative.
- Every fact you need in the argument should appear in the Statement of Facts.

## 5. Citation Format (California Courts)

### 5.1 Case Names

- Italicize case names: *Smith v. Jones*
- Short form after first citation: *Smith*, not "the Smith case" or "Smith, supra"
- Do not use "supra" for cases in California practice; repeat the short citation with volume and page.

### 5.2 California Reporters

| Reporter | Abbreviation |
|----------|-------------|
| California Reports (Supreme Court) | Cal. / Cal. 2d / Cal. 3d / Cal. 4th / Cal. 5th |
| California Appellate Reports | Cal.App. / Cal.App.2d / Cal.App.3d / Cal.App.4th / Cal.App.5th |
| California Rules Reporter | Cal. Rules of Court |

### 5.3 Statutes

Spell out the code name and use "section," not "§," in text.
- In text: `Code of Civil Procedure section 437c, subdivision (c)`
- In parenthetical: `(Code Civ. Proc., § 437c, subd. (c))`

### 5.4 Record Citations

| Source | Format |
|--------|--------|
| Clerk's Transcript | (CT [page]) |
| Reporter's Transcript | (RT [page]:[line]–[page]:[line]) |
| Appellant's Appendix | (AA [volume]:[page]) |
| Supplemental Clerk's Transcript | (SCT [page]) |

### 5.5 Federal Citations

Follow California Appellate Court rule: cite to U.S. Reports for SCOTUS decisions, Federal Reporter for circuit decisions. Always verify the reporter before filing.

## 6. Formatting

### 6.1 Sentence Length

Vary sentence length. Aim for an average of 20–25 words. Sentences over 40 words should be broken up unless structure demands length (e.g., reciting a multi-element test).

### 6.2 Paragraph Length

Court-filing paragraphs: 4–8 sentences. A single-sentence paragraph is powerful occasionally; two in a row dilutes the effect.

### 6.3 Footnotes

Use footnotes sparingly. Never footnote a point you want the court to actually read. Reserve footnotes for:
- Record citations when the text already has a citation
- Parenthetical explanations of secondary authorities
- Cross-references

## 7. Common Substitution Pairs

| Wrong | Right |
|-------|-------|
| "prior to" | "before" |
| "subsequent to" | "after" |
| "in order to" | "to" |
| "on the part of" | "by" |
| "in the event that" | "if" |
| "at the present time" | "now" |
| "due to the fact that" | "because" |
| "in close proximity to" | "near" |
| "for the purpose of" | "to" |
| "in connection with" | usually deletable; rewrite |
| "with respect to" | "on," "about," or "regarding" |
| "inasmuch as" | "because" |
| "pursuant to" | "under" (preferred in California courts) |
| "utilize" | "use" |
| "commence" | "begin" or "start" |
| "endeavor" | "try" |
| "obtain" (when "get" works) | "get" |
| "indicate" (when "say" or "show" works) | "say" or "show" |
| "assist" (when "help" works) | "help" |

## 8. Checker Scope Notes

The `klg-style-guide-check` skill reviews only the text submitted. It does not:
- Verify case citations (use `klg-cite-check` for that)
- Check record citations for accuracy (attorney verification required)
- Make judgment calls on argument strategy

All skill output is a draft for attorney review. The attorney determines whether a suggested change is appropriate for the specific filing context.
